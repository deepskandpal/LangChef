Designing a run

You are the diner, not the chef. Something else works out what the experiment has to be, what it will cost and what it can and cannot see. You order it, or you do not.

The worked example

You want to move to the replacement model. You have 90 goldens. You ask for a design and say you care about a five point change.

  langchef experiment design --suite support --target-effect 0.05

  [as-it-stands]   n=90    detects 13.2%    90 calls, $0.04
    ! These goldens cannot resolve 5.0%. The smallest effect this design
      could detect is 13.2%; a real change below that comes back inconclusive.

  [powered]        n=628   detects  5.0%   628 calls, $0.25
    ! Needs 538 more goldens than the suite has (90). Collect them before
      running, or accept the detection limit of the design above.

Neither of those is a refusal. They are two honest orders, and picking the first one knowingly is a perfectly good decision. Picking it without knowing is the thing this prevents.

Where this sits in a whole run

Designing is step nine of thirteen, and the eight before it exist to make it meaningful. The sequence, with the page that owns each part:

CommandWhat it is for
1langchef initScaffold the workspace
2collect examples50–100 from real traffic. Walked through in Start here
3write the rubricWhat "good" means, one ### per criterion. Judging
4langchef approve rubricGate one. Without it, judging refuses at exit 2
5langchef judge runScore every example. Judging and pins
6langchef label planWhich forty are worth your ten minutes. Label planning
7label them yourselfThe only part nobody can automate for you
8langchef calibrate reportIs the judge trustworthy. Agreement and taxonomy
9langchef experiment designYou are here. What the run has to be, and what it can see
10langchef experiment approveGate two. A person accepts the cost and the claim
11langchef judge run --arm variantScore the other arm, under the same pin
12langchef experiment readoutThe verdict, against the design. Comparing two arms
13langchef memo writeOne page a person can disagree with

Steps 3 to 8 are the part that has no equivalent in most eval tooling, and skipping them is what makes step 12 meaningless. If your task has a hard target, meaning you already know the right document or label, steps 3 to 8 do not apply to you at all and the run is 1, 2, 9 to 13. Which one am I?

For the same sequence as a worked tutorial with real output at every step, rather than as a map, read Start here.

Why two candidates and not one answer

The first design uses the goldens you have and tells you what they can resolve. The second exists only when you named a target the first cannot reach.

The second is usually the more useful, because "you need 628 examples and you have 90" is an answer, where a shrug is not. The common failure without it is running the underpowered version, getting "no significant difference", and reading that as "no difference". Those are not the same sentence.

The number that governs everything

How many goldens you need is driven by how small an effect you want to see, and the relationship is brutal:

  detect 20.0%   ->  n =    40
  detect 10.0%   ->  n =   157
  detect  5.0%   ->  n =   628
  detect  2.5%   ->  n =  2512

Halving the effect you want to detect costs roughly four times the examples. That is not a property of this tool, it is the shape of the arithmetic, and it is why "just collect more goldens" stops being advice quite quickly.

These are computed from the discordant rate, not the pass rate, because the comparison is paired: only examples that change verdict carry information. With no prior comparison to learn from, 20% is assumed and the design says so in discordance_source. Run the same arms once and the next design uses your real rate instead of the assumption.

What a design actually fixes

Everything that could otherwise be decided after seeing the result.

FieldWhy it is decided in advance
n, mdeSo "inconclusive" can be read as "this could never have seen it" rather than "no effect"
marginA non-inferiority tolerance chosen after seeing the interval is not a tolerance, it is a rationalisation
stopping_ruleScore everything, read out once. Stopping early when it looks good is the most common way an experiment reports an effect that is not there
guardrailsWhat must not get worse while you chase the thing you are chasing
costIn calls always, in money only if a price was configured. Inventing a price is worse than admitting the gap

Non-inferiority needs its margin up front, and refuses without one

Ask for a non-inferiority design without a margin and it will not produce one:

  a non-inferiority design needs --margin: how much quality you are
  willing to lose. Deciding it after the run is not a design.

With a three point margin on 90 goldens you get the same two-candidate shape, and a second caveat worth reading twice:

  [as-it-stands]  n=90     detects 13.2%
    ! Quality holds only if the whole interval clears −3.0%.
      The point estimate is not the test.

  [powered]       n=1745   detects  3.0%    $0.70

Approval, and why editing revokes it

An approved design is a TOML file in evals/experiments/. Its digest is computed over the body excluding the [approval] block, so:

  • approving adds a block without changing what was approved
  • editing any part of the design afterwards changes the digest and revokes the approval automatically, with nobody having to notice

You cannot widen a margin or drop a guardrail after seeing the numbers and still hold an approval. That is the whole mechanism, and it is about eight lines of code.

The gates, as exit codes

A rule in a prompt is a suggestion. An agent can talk itself past one. These are exit codes.

CodeWhenWhat it means
2reading out with no approved design, or with more than one run matching an armRefused. Reading out whichever run ran last is how an experiment gets repeated until it says something better
5the two runs were produced under different pinsRefused. You would be comparing two rulers, not two systems
4the agreed call budget ran outStopped. What was left unscored is written to undone.json

experiment check answers the question in between: does the run I have actually match the design that was approved. A run that departed from its design is marked exploratory and recommends no decision, rather than being quietly reported as a result.

src/langchef/cli/design_cmd.py — design, approve, check, readout, list
src/langchef/core/gates.py — gate one on the rubric, gate two on the experiment

What we deliberately do not do

  • No sequential or always-valid designs. The stopping rule is score everything, read out once. Peeking and stopping when it looks good inflates the false positive rate badly. Always-valid confidence sequences are the real answer to this and are not built.
  • No multi-arm designs. Two arms at a time. Sweeping five variants and reporting the winner without correction finds effects that are not there.
  • No automatic approval. An agent can produce and check a design. It cannot approve one. The approval is the point where a person accepts a cost and a claim, and automating it would remove the only human step in the loop.
  • No cost model beyond calls times a price you supply. No token estimation, no provider price list to go stale. usd is None until you configure a price, and every caller has to cope with that rather than being handed a made-up number.
  • No boolean for "is this design sensible". The payload answers a narrower question: runnable_now is true when the design can be executed with the goldens that exist, and shortfall says how many more are needed. A design needing 628 when you have 90 reports runnable_now: false and shortfall: 538. Whether it is worth collecting 538 more goldens is a judgement, and the tool declines to encode one. Past a certain size it says so in words instead: a requirement of several hundred thousand is evidence that the effect cannot be resolved this way, not a plan.

The argument for why the approval step exists at all: the distinction between generating a hypothesis from observations and testing one with new observations is not respected in practice, and the cost is credibility.
